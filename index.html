The 6 Core Characteristics of Academic & Technical Texts

Slides 8 – 26

These six characteristics form the core of Unit 2. All definitions, indicators, and examples below are quoted straight from the slide deck.

1

Evidence-Based / Objective

Slides 9, 10, 11, 12
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slides 9 & 10) "Arguments, claims, and conclusions presented in academic and technical contexts must always be supported by empirical data, reputable facts, expert insights, or primary observations.

At the same time, the writing puts more emphasis on information and arguments and less on the feelings or personal perspectives of the writer, focusing entirely on the subject matter being discussed."
Key Indicators of Evidence-Based and Objective Texts (Slide 11):
  • "Inclusion of survey results, laboratory data, statistics, and verifiable facts."
  • "Consistent use of standardized in-text citations (e.g., APA, MLA) and complete reference lists."
  • "Minimizing or eliminating first-person subjective expressions ('I think,' 'I feel,' 'in my opinion') in favor of objective phrasing."
Verbatim Slide Examples (Slide 12)
"Online learning is obviously terrible because students hate sitting in front of screens all day."
⚠️ Flaw (Slide 12): Unsupported subjective assertion relying on emotional conjecture ("hate", "obviously terrible") with zero empirical backing.
"Survey data from 250 senior high school learners reveal that 68% experienced digital fatigue, which directly correlated with a 12% decrease in self-reported engagement during extended online lectures."
✨ Why it works (Slide 12): Quantified sample size (n=250), specific percentages (68%, 12%), objective metrics, and neutral formal tone.
2

Structured

Slides 13, 14, 15
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 13) "It should follow a predictable, logical, and standardized organizational pattern. This structure serves as a roadmap that guides the reader seamlessly through complex ideas without unnecessary digressions or scatter."
Key Indicators of Structured Texts (Slide 14):
  • "Tripartite Structure for Essays: A clear Introduction (hook, background, thesis statement), Body (evidence and analysis), and Conclusion (thesis restatement, summary, clincher)."
  • "Modular / Hierarchical Setup for Technical Texts: Numbered steps, bulleted lists, clear headings, and subheadings."
Verbatim Slide Examples (Slide 15)
"Jumping straight into data results without explaining the research problem or methodology first."
⚠️ Flaw (Slide 15): Lacks introductory orientation, methodology, and predictable flow; disorients the evaluator.
"Opening with a background context and thesis statement, developing each supporting point in dedicated body paragraphs, and concluding with a clear synthesis."
✨ Why it works (Slide 15): Faithfully applies the Tripartite Structure (Intro → Body → Conclusion) to guide the reader seamlessly.
3

Critical

Slides 16, 17, 18
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 16) "Academic writing requires analytical and evaluative thinking. Writers must not merely describe or summarize information; they must develop clear, logical arguments, create depth, avoid stating the obvious, and uncover underlying insights."
Key Indicators of Critical Texts (Slide 17):
  • "Analyzing why and how a phenomenon occurs rather than just summarizing what happened."
  • "Identifying gaps, limitations, or contradictions in existing literature."
  • "Evaluating the validity, credibility, and relevance of sources before integrating them into an argument."
Verbatim Slide Examples (Slide 18)
"Author A states that social media affects study habits. Author B also writes about social media and homework."
⚠️ Flaw (Slide 18): Passive descriptive summary stating only the surface-level existence of texts without evaluation.
"While Author A attributes declining study hours directly to social media usage, Author B's findings suggest that screen time is merely a symptom of poor time management, highlighting a fundamental disagreement in how student distractibility should be conceptualized."
✨ Why it works (Slide 18): Analyzes why/how findings conflict, exposes underlying theoretical tensions, and builds intellectual depth.
4

Balanced

Slides 19, 20, 21
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 19) "Academic writing aims to consider different perspectives or arguments on an issue or topic to avoid being biased or one-sided. Writers exercise caution when making claims or conclusions to avoid making sweeping generalizations or unfounded statements."
Key Indicators of Balanced Texts (Slide 20):
  • "Acknowledging counterarguments, alternative explanations, or study limitations."
  • "Using Modal Verbs: (may, suggest, appear, seem)."
  • "Using Probability Adverbs: (likely, possibly, presumably, generally)."
  • "Using Tentative Phrases: ('the data imply that...', 'results indicate a potential link...')."
Verbatim Slide Examples (Slide 21)
"Artificial intelligence completely destroys student creativity and guarantees that no one will learn how to write."
⚠️ Flaw (Slide 21): Dogmatic absolutism ("completely destroys", "guarantees") lacking nuance and failing to consider alternate evidence.
"While the unmonitored use of AI tools may reduce independent drafting practice, preliminary findings suggest that structured AI integration can enhance student brainstorming and revision skills when guided ethically."
✨ Why it works (Slide 21): Cautious hedging using modal verbs ("may reduce", "suggest", "can enhance") and balanced consideration of both risks and ethical benefits.
5

Precise

Slides 22 & 23
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 22) "Academic and technical writing strives to provide clear, accurate, and correct information that clearly captures what the data show. Vague descriptors create ambiguity, lead to costly errors, or render scientific procedures non-replicable."
Key Indicators of Precise Texts (Slide 23):
  • "Using exact language and specific terms (e.g., using 'increase' or 'decrease' instead of merely saying the values 'changed')."
  • "Quantifying observations with exact measurements, percentages, and statistical values rather than vague modifiers."
Precision Application
"The values changed a lot after adding the solution, and many samples were ruined soon afterwards."
⚠️ Flaw: Highly ambiguous terms ("changed a lot", "many", "soon") that prevent experimental replication.
"The incubation temperature increased by 4.8°C over a 15-minute interval, resulting in the thermal degradation of 42% (n=21) of the bacterial cultures."
✨ Why it works: Uses exact terms ("increased" rather than "changed"), specific numerical values (+4.8°C), time frames (15 min), and sample proportions (42%, n=21).
6

Formal

Slides 24, 25, 26
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 24) "Academic writing observes formal conventions of writing. Adhering to formal language and tone maintains professionalism, establishes authority, and ensures respect across scholarly and technical communities."
Key Indicators of Formal Texts (Slide 25):
  • "Zero Colloquialisms, Figures of Speech, or Slang: Avoiding casual expressions (e.g., 'cool,' 'gonna,' 'trash')."
  • "No Contractions: Spelling out words completely (e.g., writing 'cannot,' 'did not,' 'would have' instead of 'can't,' 'didn't,' 'would've')."
  • "Standard Mechanics: Observing proper capitalization, punctuation, subject-verb agreement, and correct citation formatting."
Verbatim Slide Examples (Slide 26)
"The researchers couldn't figure out why the tech crashed, which was super frustrating for the team."
⚠️ Flaw (Slide 26): Uses contractions ("couldn't") and colloquial expressions ("tech crashed", "super frustrating").
"The research team was unable to identify the immediate cause of the system failure, presenting a significant technical challenge during the initial testing phase."
✨ Why it works (Slide 26): Replaces contractions with full spelling ("was unable to identify") and utilizes elevated, objective vocabulary ("system failure", "significant technical challenge").

2. Purpose (Slide 34)

Module 3: Critical Strategies for Interpreting Texts

Slides 37 – 48

Critical interpretation spans three core communication channels: Reading, Listening, and Viewing. Below are all strategies and models exactly as presented on the slides.

A. Critical Reading Strategies

Slides 38 – 41
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 38) "Critical reading involves analysis, interpretation, and evaluation. It requires moving beyond passive reading to actively question, analyze, and evaluate how an author constructs an argument."

1. Pre-Reading Strategies

Slide 39
  • • Previewing: "Reading titles, abstracts, and captions to set reading goals."
  • • SQ5R Model:
1. Survey:
"Scan headings/ visuals"
2. Question:
"Turn headings into questions"
3. Read:
"Actively seek answers"
4. Recite:
"Summarize key points"
5. Review:
"Check overall recall"

2. During Reading Strategies

Slide 40
  • • Annotation: "Highlighting main claims, circling key terms, and writing questions in the margins."
  • • TEAC Model for Paragraph Structure:
Verbatim TEAC Definitions (Slide 40):
T (Topic Sentence): "States the main claim of the paragraph."
E (Evidence): "Presents statistics, study results, or expert quotes."
A (Analysis): "Explains how the evidence proves the topic sentence."
C (Clincher/Conclusion): "Summarizes the point or transitions to the next paragraph."

3. Post-Reading Strategies

Slide 41
• Double-Entry Journaling (Slide 41):
"A two-column graphic organizer that explicitly separates text evidence from student analysis."
• Outlining (Slide 41):
"Deconstructing a text into a hierarchical alphanumeric or bulleted structure to make its organizational logic visible."
• Summarizing (Slide 41):
"Restating the central thesis and main supporting arguments of a text in your own words, removing minor details, examples, and repetitive phrasing."

B. Critical Listening Strategies

Slides 42 – 46, 48
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 42) "Critical listening in academic settings requires evaluating arguments, assessing supporting evidence, and understanding messages beyond spoken words.

Critical Listening Strategies: 1. Active Listening · 2. The Cornell Note-Taking System · 3. Reflective Listening."

1. Active Listening

Slide 43
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 43) "Focuses full cognitive attention on the speaker, filtering out distractions and withholding hasty judgments until the message is fully delivered."
How to do it (Slide 43):
  • Paying Attention: "Giving total focus to verbal cues, vocal tone, and body language."
  • Evaluating: "Analyzing the logical validity of the arguments and checking if claims are supported by facts."
  • Responding Thoughtfully: "Formulating questions or feedback based on accurate facts rather than emotional reaction."

2. The Cornell Note-Taking System

Slides 44 & 45
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 44) "A structured format for taking, organizing, and reviewing lecture notes."
How to do it (Slide 44):
Cue Column (Left, 30% width):

"Formulated immediately after the lecture; record key vocabulary terms, main themes, and potential exam questions."

Notes Column (Right, 70% width):

"Record main lecture concepts, facts, and equations using abbreviations and bullet points during the presentation."

Summary Block (Bottom, 2-3 inches):

"Write a 2-4 sentence summary in your own words synthesizing the page's core content."

3. Reflective Listening

Slides 46 & 48
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 46 & 48) "Paraphrasing or restating the speaker's core message back to them to confirm accurate interpretation before moving forward."
Verbatim Example (Slide 46 & 48): "If I understand correctly, you are saying that [paraphrased main point]. Is that correct?"

C. Critical Viewing Strategies

Slide 47
📖 Verbatim Definition (Slide 47) "Critical viewing empowers learners to decode, analyze, and evaluate multimodal visuals (charts, diagrams, infographics, and videos) to detect bias, identify manipulated data, and verify claims."
What to look out for (Slide 47):
1. Visual Grammar:

Graphic hierarchy, framing, color psychology, and symbolic iconography used to direct viewer emphasis.

2. Scale Integrity:

Ensuring chart axes begin at zero with proportional, uniform intervals to prevent exaggerated distortions.

3. Source Credibility:

Evaluating dataset recency, publication credibility, sampling integrity, and checking for commercial/sponsor bias.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Cram Sheet (Verbatim Essentials)

Exact definitions and rules from the 48 slides compiled for rapid last-minute memorization.

Academic vs Technical Texts (Slides 4–7)

  • Academic Text: "Formal, structured, linear, and evidence-based writing used in scholarly settings to analyze ideas, construct arguments, or build knowledge." (Goal: inform, analyze, persuade).
  • Technical Text: "Functional, practical, and highly precise writing designed to instruct, direct, or document procedures so readers can perform tasks safely and efficiently." (Goal: guide action, explain process, document data).
  • Shared Umbrella (Slide 8): Formal, informative communication.

The 6 Characteristics (Slides 8–26)

  • 1. Evidence-Based/Objective: Supported by empirical data/facts; zero first-person feelings ("I think", "I feel").
  • 2. Structured: Tripartite Structure (Intro, Body, Conclusion); Technical = Modular (numbered steps, subheadings).
  • 3. Critical: Analytical depth; asks *why and how*, finds literature gaps, evaluates source validity.
  • 4. Balanced: Multi-perspective, avoids sweeping claims. Hedging: modal verbs (*may, suggest*), adverbs (*likely, possibly*), tentative phrases.
  • 5. Precise: Exact data (use *increased/decreased* instead of *changed*); quantifies numbers, units, and percentages.
  • 6. Formal: Zero slang/colloquialisms, no contractions (spell out *cannot, did not*), standard mechanics.

The APC Framework (Slides 27–36)

  • Audience (Slide 29): Dictates level of detail, tone, and vocabulary.
    • Expert (Slide 30): Use specialized technical jargon directly without defining basic concepts.
    • Non-Expert / Lay (Slide 31): Simplify terms, use relatable everyday analogies, define terms immediately.
    • Peer (Slide 32): Balance professional rigor with engaging, relatable explanations.
    • Academic Evaluator (Slide 33): Demonstrate complete mastery, rigorous citation, and strict formal structure.
  • Purpose (Slide 34): 1. To Inform / Explain, 2. To Persuade / Argue, 3. To Instruct / Direct.
  • Context (Slide 35 & 36): Setting, Medium/Format, Discipline Expectations (Humanities = argument/textual evidence; Sciences = methodology/replicable data).

Critical Strategies (Slides 37–48)

  • Reading Pre (Slide 39): Previewing & SQ5R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review).
  • Reading During (Slide 40): Annotation & TEAC (Topic sentence, Evidence, Analysis, Clincher).
  • Reading Post (Slide 41): Double-Entry Journaling (text evidence vs student analysis), Outlining, Summarizing.
  • Listening Active (Slide 43): Paying Attention, Evaluating, Responding Thoughtfully.
  • Listening Cornell (Slide 44): Left Cue (30% width), Right Notes (70% width), Bottom Summary (2-3 inches).
  • Listening Reflective (Slide 46): Paraphrasing: *"If I understand correctly, you are saying that [paraphrase]. Is that correct?"*
  • Viewing (Slide 47): Visual Grammar, Scale Integrity (zero baseline), Source Credibility.
